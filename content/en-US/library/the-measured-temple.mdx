**Scripture: Revelation 11:1–2 | Sunday Sermon, November 8, 2026 | Pastor Chongzhu (Paul) Zhang**

> In Revelation 11:1–2, John receives a measuring rod and is told to measure God's temple, the altar, and the worshipers. He is told to leave out the outer court, which has been given to the nations; they will trample the holy city for forty-two months. *(Scripture summarized; see Revelation 11:1–2.)*

## Introduction: Why Does God Command John to Measure?

Revelation 10 tells us that John receives and eats a little scroll. It tastes sweet in his mouth but turns bitter in his stomach. In chapter 11, God gives John a reed as a measuring rod and commands him to measure the temple, the altar, and those who worship there—but not the outer court. The nations will also trample the holy city for forty-two months.

This vision confronts us with three questions: **Who truly belongs to God? What are we really relying on? And when suffering comes, who can enable us to stand firm to the end?**

## 1. Measuring the Temple: Who Truly Belongs to God? (11:1)

In Scripture, measuring can relate to God's purposes, ownership, judgment, or restoration. Ezekiel 40–43 describes the temple being measured in detail; Zechariah 2 portrays the measuring of Jerusalem; and Amos 7 uses a plumb line in a message of judgment. These visions do not all mean exactly the same thing. Each must be understood in context. Yet they all show that what God measures matters deeply.

Revelation 11:1 is striking because John is commanded to measure **not only the temple and altar, but also the worshipers**. Our attention moves from a building to the people within it, and from outward religion to a living relationship with God.

Our congregation is currently looking for a suitable place to gather. We examine square footage, price, location, parking, and the arrangement of the rooms. These practical concerns matter. A space for worship, Sunday school, discipleship training, and fellowship can serve the church well. But the size of a building does not prove spiritual maturity. Attendance, offerings, and the number of activities cannot replace faithfulness to Christ.

Money can build a church building, but it cannot purchase salvation. Religious activity can bring a person recognition, but it cannot conceal sin from God. True worship means more than entering a religious building: it means coming to Jesus Christ in faith and responding to God with our lives.

Paul reminds us, “The Lord knows those who are his” (2 Timothy 2:19). These words bring both comfort and warning. They comfort us because the Lord never forgets his people. They warn us because religious identity alone does not guarantee a genuine relationship with him. In the same way, the words “God is with us” comfort those who trust him, while also calling us to reverence in what we say and do.

We must therefore ask ourselves: Do I come to worship because I truly trust Christ, or merely from habit, concern for appearances, or personal advantage? God cares not only where we sit but whether our hearts belong to him.

## 2. Leaving Out the Outer Court: What Are We Really Relying On? (11:2a)

The passage then says that the outer court is to be left unmeasured because it has been given to the nations. In the historical temple, the courts and sanctuary had distinct ritual functions. Revelation, however, speaks here in visionary language. We should not conclude that worshipers today belong to fixed spiritual classes, nor should we equate Gentile identity with exclusion from God's people. The New Testament teaches that both Jews and Gentiles have access to the Father through Christ in one Spirit (Ephesians 2:18–22).

At the very least, the vision reminds us that **visible religious space is not our ultimate security**. The outer court is handed over, and the holy city will be trampled. The outward structures people depend on can be shaken.

For our church, this is not an abstract lesson. We have been searching for a permanent meeting place. Brothers and sisters have prayed earnestly and given generously. One sister who had never attended our congregation heard about our need for a building and, together with her family, contributed fifty thousand dollars. We thank God for such love. Yet some properties we hoped to purchase were sold to others, and we are still waiting for a suitable arrangement. In disappointment, I have prayed, “Lord, why have we prayed so earnestly and still not found our own church building?”

Through this waiting, God's Word keeps reminding me: **A church building is a tool for ministry, not the church's savior. Christ alone is the head of the church.** Money can support ministry but cannot produce spiritual life. A building can provide a gathering place but cannot replace Christ's presence. Jesus says that true worshipers worship the Father in spirit and truth (John 4:23).

If we do not yet have our own building, will we still love one another, proclaim the gospel faithfully, and make disciples? If our resources decrease, will our devotion to the Lord remain strong? Many churches began with only a few believers meeting in a home. What sustains the church is Christ's grace, God's Word, the work of the Holy Spirit, and the love believers share.

So we will continue looking carefully for a meeting place without placing our faith in real estate. We will continue giving thanks for offerings without measuring God's pleasure by the amount received. May the Lord use this season of waiting to refine us, so that those who continue to worship and serve will treasure one another and answer his call faithfully.

## 3. Forty-Two Months of Trampling: Who Enables Us to Stand in Suffering? (11:2b)

The text says that the nations “will trample the holy city for forty-two months.” The startling point is that God commands the temple to be measured while allowing the holy city to be trampled. This does not mean that God has lost his sovereignty, nor does it mean that his people will never suffer. Revelation soon describes two witnesses who testify for the Lord and are even killed (Revelation 11:3–10). God's protection does not mean believers can never face persecution, loss, or death. It means that suffering cannot overturn his final promise of redemption.

Forty-two months corresponds to 1,260 days when reckoned as thirty-day months, or three and a half years. Revelation repeatedly uses related time expressions (see Revelation 11:3; 12:6, 14; 13:5), echoing apocalyptic imagery in Daniel. Christians differ in how they identify the specific historical referents of these numbers. Yet the pastoral point here remains powerful: **the powers opposed to God are not unlimited, and the season of suffering is not outside his rule**. This does not promise that every personal trial will end within three and a half years.

When someone faces unemployment, illness, family breakdown, or pressure against the church, the pain is real and the waiting can be long. Scripture does not ask us to dismiss our tears. Instead, it directs our eyes to the Lord who endured the cross and rose from the dead.

Jesus was crucified at about nine in the morning (Mark 15:25). Darkness covered the land from noon until three in the afternoon (Mark 15:33), and Jesus then breathed his last (Mark 15:37). To human eyes, the cross looked like defeat. Yet through Christ's death and resurrection, God accomplished salvation. The resurrection declares that death does not have the final word, and darkness is not the end of history.

Paul therefore asks, “Who shall separate us from the love of Christ?” (Romans 8:35). His answer is that neither hardship, distress, persecution, nor anything else in all creation can separate us from God's love in Christ Jesus (Romans 8:35–39). Our hope is not that circumstances will always be favorable. Our hope is that the risen Christ remains faithful.

## Conclusion: Living as People Who Truly Belong to the Lord

Revelation 11:1–2 invites us to respond in three ways.

**First, examine our belonging.** As God measures the temple and its worshipers, we ask: Do I truly trust Christ and obey his Word?

**Second, correct our dependence.** As the outer court is left out, we ask: Have I made buildings, money, attendance, or religious forms my ultimate security?

**Third, hold fast to hope.** As the holy city is trampled, we ask: When suffering comes, will I still follow the crucified and risen Lord faithfully?

We will keep searching for an appropriate meeting place and praying for the growth of our church. But may we never use outward numbers as proof of spiritual health. Instead, may we respond to God's grace with faith, holiness, love, and perseverance. **The Lord knows those who are his; the true foundation of the church is Jesus Christ.**

## Closing Prayer

Loving, holy, and righteous heavenly Father, we thank and praise you. Shine the light of your truth upon us, so that we will not depend merely on religious forms, attendance, offerings, or buildings, but will trust Jesus Christ wholeheartedly. You know the waiting, hopes, and disappointments our congregation has experienced as we search for a meeting place. Guard every brother and sister. Whatever our numbers and however our circumstances change, help us to love one another, worship faithfully, and proclaim the gospel diligently.

When we become proud, discouraged, or weak in faith, forgive and strengthen us. Remind us that Christ is always the head of the church, and establish our faith upon him, our rock. Even in suffering, keep us steadfast through his resurrection and unfailing love. To you be all glory. We pray in the holy name of Jesus Christ. Amen.
