# The Cry Beneath the Altar --- When God Seems Silent, the Saints Still Trust His Reign

**Scripture: Revelation 6:9--11**

**Big Idea: The believer's lament is not unbelief. It entrusts vengeance
to the righteous God and remains faithful while waiting.**

## Introduction: May Christians Ask "Why?"

Some people think faith means never asking questions.

When suffering comes, they think we may only say, "Praise the Lord."
When we are hurt, we should not express grief. When we see injustice, we
should simply remain silent.

But when the fifth seal is opened, we hear martyrs crying loudly before
God:

> "How long, O Lord, holy and true, dost thou not judge and avenge our
> blood on them that dwell on the earth?" (Rev. 6:10, KJV)

Scripture does not rebuke them. Their cry is preserved within the
heavenly vision.

## I. Lives Beneath the Altar --- Faithful Witness on the Way of the Lamb (6:9)

John sees "the souls of them that were slain for the word of God, and
for the testimony which they held" beneath the altar.

In the Old Testament sacrificial system, blood was poured at the base of
the altar (see Lev. 4:7), and Leviticus 17:11 associates the life of the
flesh with the blood. The imagery therefore presents these martyrs'
lives as lives poured out before God.

Why beneath the altar?

Their lives have been joined to the way of the Lamb.

The biblical movement is suggestive:

Leviticus → altar → cross → the altar in Revelation.

But we must be clear: the martyrs are not another atoning sacrifice.
Christ alone atones for sin. They participate in the Lamb's way because
faithfulness to Him has brought suffering.

Peter says that Christ suffered for us, leaving us an example so that we
should follow His steps (1 Pet. 2:21). He also speaks of rejoicing
insofar as believers share Christ's sufferings (1 Pet. 4:13). Paul longs
to know Christ, the power of His resurrection, and the fellowship of His
sufferings (Phil. 3:10).

These martyrs did not die because of personal ambition or an ordinary
quarrel. They were slain "for the word of God" and "for the testimony
which they held."

That echoes John's own situation in Revelation 1:9, where he says he was
on Patmos "for the word of God, and for the testimony of Jesus Christ."

The church must distinguish suffering for the gospel from suffering
caused by our own pride, harshness, or wrongdoing.

Peter warns believers not to suffer as murderers, thieves, evildoers, or
busybodies, but says that if anyone suffers as a Christian, that person
should not be ashamed (see 1 Pet. 4:15--16).

### Application

Not every form of suffering is a cross. Sometimes the problem is our own
flesh.

We must not call every disagreement persecution. Someone objecting to
our attitude is not necessarily opposing the gospel. Someone correcting
our error is not necessarily persecuting the church.

True Christian witness is paying a cost because we remain faithful to
God's Word and to Christ.

The tears that belong beneath God's altar are tears shed in faithfulness
to Christ.

## II. The Cry Beneath the Altar --- A Prayer of Faith to the Sovereign Lord (6:10)

The martyrs call God "O Lord, holy and true," and then ask, "How long?"

The word translated "Lord" is **δεσπότης (Despotēs)**. In this context
it emphasizes the Lord as sovereign Master, the One possessing supreme
authority over history.

Their question is therefore not simply, "God, why?" It is more like:

"Lord, because You rule over all things, how long until You act?"

"How long?" is familiar language in the Psalms and the Prophets.

> "How long wilt thou forget me, O LORD? for ever?" (Ps. 13:1, KJV)

Habakkuk also cries:

> "O LORD, how long shall I cry, and thou wilt not hear!" (Hab. 1:2,
> KJV)

Biblical lament is not abandoning faith. It is refusing to leave God in
the middle of pain.

True faith is not having no questions.

True faith is bringing our questions to God.

Unbelief may turn away from God in suffering. Faith clings to God in
suffering.

The martyrs do not avenge themselves. They ask God to judge. This is
consistent with Romans 12:19:

> "Dearly beloved, avenge not yourselves... for it is written, Vengeance
> is mine; I will repay, saith the Lord." (KJV)

So do not retaliate, but do not pretend the wound is unreal either.
Bring the case to God.

The martyrs are not doubting whether God is holy. They cry precisely
because they believe He is holy and true, and therefore evil cannot
remain forever unanswered.

When we are hurt, we often move toward one of two wrong responses.

One is suppression: "Nothing happened. I am fine."

The other is revenge: "I want them to hurt the way I hurt."

Revelation gives us a third way: lament before God, bring the case
before Him, and entrust judgment to Him.

You may pray:

"Lord, this is unjust."\
"Lord, I am truly hurt."\
"Lord, I do not know how long I must wait."\
"But I believe You are holy and true. I will not take revenge into my
own hands. I entrust justice to You."

## III. The White Robe Beneath the Altar --- Heaven's Vindication and Final Hope (6:11)

Each martyr receives a white robe and is told to rest "yet for a little
season," until the appointed course involving their fellow servants and
brothers is complete.

The white robe is more than emotional comfort. Within Revelation's
imagery, white garments are associated with acceptance, victory, and the
vindication of God's people.

On earth they appear defeated. In heaven they are received as victors.

The contrast is powerful:

Earthly courts may condemn; heaven vindicates.\
Earth may shame; heaven gives honor.\
Earth may strip away dignity; heaven clothes God's people in white.

Then comes the difficult word: wait.

God does not immediately explain every reason. He does not end history
at once.

His answer is, in effect:

"I have received you."\
"Your faithfulness has not been forgotten."\
"Judgment will come."\
"But rest a little while longer."

The language about the completion of the number should not become a
speculative calculation. The emphasis is that God's redemptive history
is moving toward its appointed completion.

Second Peter reminds believers that what some call delay is not
forgetfulness; God's patience serves His saving purpose, and the day of
the Lord will come (see 2 Pet. 3:8--10).

God's patience is not amnesia.

Sometimes God does not immediately change our circumstances. Instead,
the vision gives us a white robe and the words, "Rest yet for a little
season."

The white robe says: your identity and God's verdict are secure.

The waiting says: God's timetable is not finished.

In prolonged illness, years of prayer for family, difficult ministry,
and experiences of injustice, believers do not need cheap answers. We
need to know:

God sees.\
God remembers.\
God will judge justly.\
Faithfulness to Him is never wasted.

## Conclusion

Many people today feel that the world belongs to Caesar---to coercive
power, violence, and those who can impose their will.

But the saints beneath the altar tell another story.

Empire does not have the final word.\
Tyrants do not write the final verdict.\
The One on the throne possesses final judgment.

**Earthly courts can misjudge; heaven's court will not.**

**The world may be silent for a time; God will not remain silent
forever.**

The white robe is given.\
Judgment will come.\
The Lamb will finally triumph.

Today we may still live inside the question, "How long?"

But one day that cry will become a song of "Hallelujah," because the
tears beneath the altar will finally become everlasting praise before
the throne of the Lamb.
