# Was It Really Samuel? A Study of 1 Samuel 28

**Text: 1 Samuel 28:3–25**  
**Author: Paul Zhang**

## Introduction

In 1 Samuel 28:14, the medium at Endor describes an old man coming up, wearing a robe, and Saul identifies him as Samuel. Christian interpreters have offered two main explanations: God exceptionally permitted Samuel himself to appear, or a deceiving spirit impersonated Samuel. I lean toward the second interpretation, while distinguishing what the text explicitly states from theological inference.

## 1. Historical Context: Saul Turns to a Forbidden Practice

Samuel had died, the Philistines were gathering, and Saul was afraid. He inquired of the Lord but received no answer through dreams, the Urim, or prophets (1 Sam. 28:3–6). He then sought a medium (28:7). Samuel had previously rebuked Saul for disobeying God and announced that the kingdom would be taken from him (15:22–28). The chapter primarily exposes Saul's disobedience; it does not teach a method of communicating with the dead.

## 2. God Explicitly Forbids Necromancy

Deuteronomy 18:10–12 forbids divination, mediums, and consulting the dead. Leviticus 19:31 likewise warns God's people not to turn to mediums. These commands establish that necromancy is not an approved means of seeking divine guidance. Yet the prohibition alone does not logically prove that God could never permit Samuel to appear in an exceptional event. The moral prohibition and the question of the figure's identity must be distinguished.

## 3. The Dead Are Not at the Command of the Living: The Rich Man and Lazarus

In Luke 16:19–31, the rich man asks that Lazarus cross the great chasm to help him and later asks that Lazarus be sent to warn his five brothers. Abraham points to the fixed chasm and says that the brothers have Moses and the Prophets to hear (Luke 16:26, 29). This teaching indicates that the condition of the dead is not subject to the wishes of the living, and that people should heed God's revealed Word rather than seek messages from the dead.

Nevertheless, Luke 16 does not directly interpret the Endor incident, nor does it establish that God could never cause a deceased person to appear. The Transfiguration (Matt. 17:1–3) reminds us to distinguish God's sovereign initiative from human necromancy.

## 4. Spiritual Deception and Disguise

Second Corinthians 11:14 says that Satan disguises himself as an angel of light, and John 8:44 identifies the devil with falsehood. An impersonating spirit is therefore a theologically possible explanation. Possibility, however, is not proof that this particular figure was a spirit. Supernatural appearances must be evaluated by God's Word, not merely by their impressive appearance or mysterious character.

## 5. How Did Saul Identify the Figure?

In 1 Samuel 28:13–14, the medium reports what she sees; Saul asks about its appearance, and she describes an old man wearing a robe. Saul then concludes that the figure is Samuel. The text does not explicitly state that Saul saw the figure himself. This allows room for the impersonation interpretation.

On the other hand, the narrator directly calls the figure “Samuel” in 28:15–16 and 20. That is significant textual evidence for the view that Samuel truly appeared. The impersonation interpretation must address it rather than dismiss it. Reading the designation as reflecting Saul's understanding is possible, but it is not expressly stated by the text.

## 6. Does the Fulfilled Prediction Establish Identity?

The figure repeats the judgment against Saul and predicts the death of Saul and his sons (1 Sam. 28:16–19). Saul and several of his sons subsequently die in battle (1 Sam. 31). Those who affirm a genuine appearance regard the fulfilled prediction as evidence of prophetic authority.

Those who favor impersonation suggest that a deceiving spirit might know certain facts or exploit earlier revelations and the military situation. Deuteronomy 13:1–3 also warns that even an apparently fulfilled sign does not validate guidance that leads people away from God. Yet Scripture does not explain how a deceiving spirit could know the precise prediction here. We must not attribute divine omniscience to evil spirits. This remains a genuine difficulty for the impersonation view.

## 7. Chronicles' Judgment on Saul

First Chronicles 10:13–14 connects Saul's death with his unfaithfulness, his disobedience, and his consultation of a medium. This clearly establishes the moral verdict: Saul sinned in seeking such guidance. It does not directly identify the figure that appeared, so it should not be treated as conclusive evidence on that separate question.

## 8. The Two Interpretations and Their Difficulties

**The genuine-Samuel view:** The narrator repeatedly names Samuel, the message agrees with the earlier judgment, and the prediction is fulfilled. This interpretation attributes the appearance to God's exceptional permission, not to the medium's control over a prophet. Its challenge is to explain why God would allow this appearance in the context of a forbidden practice.

**The impersonation view:** God's prohibition of necromancy, the inability of the living to command the dead, the possibility of demonic disguise, and Saul's reliance on the medium's description all support caution about identifying the figure as Samuel. Its challenges are the narrator's direct naming of Samuel and the accurate prediction of Saul's death.

## 9. Conclusion and Spiritual Application

Considering the broader biblical teaching, **I lean toward the interpretation that a deceiving spirit impersonated Samuel**, but I do not present it as an explicit and undisputed conclusion of the passage. Whichever view one adopts, several truths remain clear: God forbids consulting the dead; the dead are not subject to a medium's control; divine revelation does not depend on necromancy; and believers must heed God's Word.

Saul's deepest tragedy was not merely the absence of a new answer but his persistent refusal to obey what God had already revealed. Abraham's words in Luke 16:29—that they have Moses and the Prophets—remain a timely reminder. We must not replace Scripture with mystical experiences, or repentance, trust, and obedience with a pursuit of supernatural phenomena.

**Central lesson: Genuine faith is not an endless search for supernatural experiences, but knowing, believing, and obeying the Word of God.**
