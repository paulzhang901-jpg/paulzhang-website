# Let's Talk About Studying Theology Today

**By Muchangke (Paul Zhang)**

A few days ago, I wrote an article titled *How Can You Come to the
United States to Study Theology?*

My original intention was simply to organize some of my experiences,
observations, and practical information from over the years as a
reference for those who are considering theological training. But after
the article was published, the comments raised another question that is
well worth discussing:

If a person wants to serve God, does he or she need to study theology?

One reader said, "I like pastors who have faith but also understand real
life. When I was a student, I met some pastors whom I regarded as
teachers. I felt they had plenty of principles to teach, but not much
practical guidance."

Another said, "I know seminary graduates and ministers whose personal
lives of faith do not seem to radiate the Father's light."

Another reader asked even more directly: "Why should a poor pastor spend
so much money and make such a sacrifice to study theology in the United
States? Is it for the title and prestige? To escape the present
environment? Most study abroad in Europe and America is tied to economic
investment... As for academic training, that is simply an academic
career path---a way to make a living within Christianity."

I read these comments carefully.

I do not really think these questions are "against theology." On the
contrary, they raise several issues that have existed in Christian
communities for a long time and deserve serious thought:

What is the relationship between academic credentials and spiritual
life? Between seminary and the church? Between knowledge and ministry?
And between calling and the path a person chooses?

So today I do not want to talk about "how to come to the United States
to study theology." I want to talk about one thing: Why study theology?

Or, more precisely: What does it actually mean to study theology?

## 1. Let Me Begin with a Conclusion: You Can Absolutely Be Used by God Without Attending Seminary

This is the first thing I want to make clear.

If someone asks me, "Do I have to attend seminary in order to become a
good minister?"

My answer is: Of course not.

If a seminary diploma were a prerequisite for serving God, Peter would
probably have been disqualified from the beginning.

Peter was originally a fisherman from Galilee.

Acts 4 tells us that when the members of the council saw the boldness of
Peter and John and realized that they were "unlearned and ignorant men,"
they marveled. But the next statement is extremely important: "they took
knowledge of them, that they had been with Jesus."

That was another kind of education.

Peter never sat in what we would later call a seminary classroom, but he
followed Jesus for more than three years.

He listened to Jesus preach. He watched Jesus heal. He saw how Jesus
dealt with crowds and how He handled disputes among His disciples. He
witnessed the feeding of the five thousand and experienced storms at
sea. He failed, denied the Lord three times, and then saw the risen
Christ with his own eyes.

This was classic apprenticeship.

It was not simply taking a course in a classroom. It was living
alongside a teacher.

You watch how your teacher prays.

You follow how your teacher serves.

You experience how your teacher faces suffering.

And finally, the teacher entrusts the mission to you:

"Follow me."

So throughout Christian history, formal academic education has never
been the only path.

## 2. On the Other Hand, Systematic Spiritual Training Has Also Existed from Ancient Times

However, if we therefore reach another conclusion---"Peter never
attended seminary, so theological education is unnecessary"---we have
simply gone to the opposite extreme.

In fact, Scripture itself already shows us forms of organized spiritual
training.

In Samuel's time, a company of prophets gathered at Naioth in Ramah,
with Samuel standing among them as their leader (1 Samuel 19).

By the time of Elijah and Elisha, we repeatedly encounter groups known
as the "sons of the prophets."

They lived together and received instruction together. When their
numbers grew and their living quarters became too small, they went
together to the Jordan to cut timber and build a larger place. It was
there that an axe head fell into the water and Elisha caused the sunken
iron to float (2 Kings 6).

Of course, we should not simply equate these communities with modern
seminaries.

But they do show us at least this much: organized training for ministry,
carried on within a tradition and under the guidance of teachers, is not
a modern invention.

Scripture itself contains two important patterns of formation:

One is apprenticeship---learning by following a teacher.

The other is a more systematic form of learning and communal training.

The same is true today.

Some people may be best trained by spending ten or twenty years
alongside a mature pastor in the local church, learning little by little
through visitation, preaching, prayer, leadership, and pastoral care.

Others may need to enter seminary and systematically study Scripture,
theology, church history, Greek, Hebrew, missiology, hermeneutics,
ethics, and pastoral ministry.

There is no need for these two paths to deny each other.

## 3. Peter and Paul Themselves Represent Two Very Different Paths

One fascinating contrast in the New Testament is the difference between
Peter and Paul.

Peter came from a fishing background.

Most of his formation came through following Jesus.

Paul was very different.

He said that he was "brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel,
and taught according to the perfect manner of the law of the fathers"
(Acts 22:3).

In other words, Paul was an intellectual who had received rigorous
training by the standards of his time.

He knew the Old Testament. He knew Jewish tradition. He was also able to
enter the Greco-Roman world and engage people from different cultures.

Later, on the road to Damascus, he encountered the risen Christ and
received his calling. He also went into Arabia and passed through a
period in which his life and understanding were being reordered.

So we see two men:

One was a fisherman.

The other was a scholar.

One grew primarily by following a master.

The other had received highly systematic training.

One later lived mainly through gospel ministry.

The other often worked with his own hands, even making tents.

Paul himself said that his own hands had provided for his needs and for
those who were with him.

And yet here is the fascinating thing: God used both of them.

Scripture never tells us that there is only one model for a minister.

God does not run an assembly line.

He does not shape every person into exactly the same form.

## 4. Academic Credentials Are Not Spiritual Life, but They Can Be a "Door Opener"

One reader commented, "Graduating from seminary does not mean that a
person has a good spiritual life."

I completely agree.

Studying theology does not automatically mean spiritual maturity.

Having a doctoral degree does not mean loving the Lord more.

Knowing Greek does not mean knowing how to love people better.

Being able to explain predestination does not mean having greater
patience.

Being able to teach the Trinity does not guarantee a healthy marriage.

Being able to write a twenty-page paper does not mean knowing when to
remain silent in the presence of someone who is weak and hurting.

Theological knowledge cannot replace spiritual life.

But the reverse is also true: spiritual enthusiasm cannot replace
necessary preparation.

A person may deeply love the Lord and still not automatically know how
to interpret Scripture correctly.

A person may be passionate and still mistake personal experience for
truth.

A person may be excellent at sharing testimonies and still interpret a
verse apart from its context.

That is why I often think academic credentials cannot prove everything
about a person, but they can provide an initial point of reference.

Suppose a complete stranger comes into a church and says:

"I am a minister."

We do not yet know his life, family, character, or ministry.

How do we begin to understand who he is?

Where was he trained?

Who taught him?

What is his theological tradition?

What has he studied?

What ministry experience does he have?

These questions can help us make an initial assessment.

That is why I often say:

Academic credentials do not equal ability, but they can often serve as a
door opener.\
A diploma does not equal spiritual life, but it does at least indicate
that a person has undergone some form of systematic training.

Of course, that is only the beginning, not the conclusion.

In the end, what truly demonstrates who a person is will never be the
diploma hanging on the wall, but the fruit that his or her life bears
over the decades.

## 5. Why, Then, Do Some Seminary Graduates Still Have Poor Spiritual Lives?

There is really nothing surprising about this.

A school can train the mind, but it cannot obey God on someone's behalf.

A seminary can require you to study Romans, but it cannot force you to
live out Romans.

A professor can teach the theology of the cross, but cannot carry your
cross for you.

A professor can explain humility, but cannot make you lay down your
pride in the middle of a conflict.

Seminary provides an environment, knowledge, teachers, traditions,
training, and resources.

How a person responds to all of that is another matter.

Jesus personally trained twelve disciples.

And yet among those twelve, was there not a Judas?

Would we therefore say:

"Jesus' discipleship training did not work"?

Of course not.

So when we see several seminary graduates whose lives are unhealthy and
conclude that theological education has no value, the logic does not
hold.

Likewise, seeing one person who never formally studied theology serve
with great effectiveness does not prove that no one needs systematic
training.

Individual cases cannot replace sound principles.

## 6. Why, Then, Would Someone Pay Such a High Price to Study Theology?

This may be the most practical question raised in the comments.

Especially: "Why does it have to be in the United States?"

My answer is still: It does not.

Theological education does not exist only in America.

Today there are many forms of theological training in China, Asia,
Europe, North America, and elsewhere. There are also online courses,
church-based theological education, mentoring systems, short-term
programs, and many platforms for cross-cultural learning.

If someone already has an excellent mentor, thorough training, and a
clear ministry path within his or her own church, there is absolutely no
need to spend money that person cannot afford simply for the sake of
"studying abroad."

Nor is there any need to turn "an American seminary" into a form of
spiritual branding.

If studying theology is merely about adding another title, I do not
think it means very much.

If the goal is simply to have people call you "Doctor," the cost may be
far too high.

But for some people, overseas theological education genuinely has a
particular significance.

For example:

A person may be called to cross-cultural ministry.

He may need exposure to different church traditions.

He may need to learn another language.

He may be preparing for future academic research.

He may be called to train the next generation of ministers.

He may need to understand the global church.

Or perhaps God uses a season away from familiar surroundings to broaden
his vision and prune his life.

For that person, those years are not merely about "getting a degree."

They may be part of his life's calling and mission.

## 7. The Real Question Is Never Simply "Should I Study?" but "Why Am I Studying?"

I increasingly believe that we should not turn this issue into two
opposing camps.

One side says, "You need theological education to be professional."

The other says, "Truly spiritual people do not need theology at all."

Both statements are too simplistic.

The real questions are:

Where has God placed you?\
What gifts has He given you?\
What mission is He preparing you to carry?\
What preparation do you still lack for that mission?

If you are called to be a faithful Christian businessperson, then
running your business responsibly, dealing honestly with people, caring
for employees, and living out the gospel in the marketplace may be your
field of ministry.

If you are called to pastor a church over the long term, systematic
preparation in Scripture, theology, pastoral ministry, and church
history may be extremely important.

If you are called to cross-cultural missions, you may need to study
language, anthropology, and culture.

If you are called to theological research or seminary education,
master's and doctoral degrees and years of academic training may well be
unavoidable.

Different missions require different forms of preparation.

Not everyone needs the same pair of shoes, because God has not asked
everyone to walk the same road.

## 8. The True Goal of Theology Is Not Simply to Know More, but to Know God More Deeply

There is one more thing I want to say.

When we talk about "studying theology," we must not reduce theology to a
collection of abstract ideas.

The word "theology" itself is concerned with the knowledge of God.

True theology should not make a person increasingly proud, but
increasingly reverent.

It should not merely make a person better at arguing, but more aware of
his own limitations.

It should not simply add information to the mind, but help a person know
God more accurately, interpret Scripture more faithfully, and serve
others more humbly.

If someone becomes more arrogant the more theology he studies, the
problem is not that his theology is too deep. It is that his life has
not kept pace with what he is learning.

But if someone refuses serious study because he is afraid that
"knowledge puffeth up," that is not humility either.

True humility says:

I know I am limited, so I am willing to learn.\
I know I may be wrong, so I am willing to be corrected.\
I know Scripture is precious, so I dare not interpret it carelessly.\
I know the responsibility of ministry is great, so I am willing to
prepare myself for the mission.

## 9. So, Should You Study Theology?

If you ask me:

"Pastor Zhang, should I study theology?"

I probably would not immediately answer, "Yes."

Nor would I immediately answer, "No."

I would first ask you one question:

Are you clear about the calling God has given you?

Because what ultimately determines the path a person should take ought
not to be academic credentials, trends, or what other people think.

You should not pursue a doctorate simply because someone else is doing
one.

You should not decide that you need no theological training simply
because someone else serves effectively without it.

And you should not travel thousands of miles to the United States merely
because an American seminary sounds impressive.

The real question is: What has God entrusted to you?

If He asks you to stay, then stay faithfully.

If He asks you to go, then go courageously.

If He asks you to work, then work well.

If He calls you into full-time ministry, then serve faithfully.

If He asks you to sit in a classroom for five years, then study
diligently.

If He asks you to follow an older pastor for ten years, then follow
humbly.

What matters most in life is not copying someone else's path, but
completing the path God has entrusted to you.

So when it comes to studying theology, I return to this:

Academic credentials are not a calling, and seminary is not the goal.\
What matters is first hearing the call clearly, and then preparing
yourself for that call.

Whether you study theology or not;

whether you remain in China or come to the United States;

whether your formation is academic or apprenticeship-based;

whether you serve full-time or while holding another job---

if that is the road God has entrusted to you,

walk it faithfully.

Because one day, the question for which we will truly give an account
before Him will not be:

"What degree did you earn?"

But rather:

"What I entrusted to you---were you faithful?"
