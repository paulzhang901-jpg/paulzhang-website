# What Are We Trading For What?
### A candid conversation about money, work, and what lasts

*by Paul Zhang*

A joke has been making the rounds online. If you take some of the most tender lines from classical Chinese poetry — verses written about love — and swap the word "love" for the word "money," the lines still scan perfectly. They still rhyme. They still ache.

> What is love, that people would live and die for it?
> With money, friends will travel a thousand miles to see you.
> Without it, they won't cross the street.

*(riffing on classical Chinese love poetry — the sharpest version swaps a famous line about a farewell gift of friendship for one about a gift of cash)*

It's funny. And then, a beat later, it isn't. The joke lands because it's true. Money has worked its way into nearly every seam of modern life — school costs money, housing costs money, medicine costs money, growing old costs money, weddings cost money, children cost money, even leaving town for a weekend costs money. Most of us open our eyes in the morning and are already, somehow, doing business with it.

So today, I want to talk about money. Honestly, and without flinching.

⸻

## I. Were We Born Loving Money?

People sometimes say the love of money runs in the blood of a culture — mine included. I don't think that's quite fair. Read the old texts seriously, and you find that no serious tradition ever placed money at the top of the human story.

There's an old Chinese saying: a gentleman may love wealth, but he earns it by the right road. The weight of that sentence was never on the loving — it was on the road. Money can be earned, but not all of it should be. Wealth can be pursued, but never at the cost of who you are. A person can be poor and still have backbone. A person can have very little money and still refuse to live without integrity, without family, without friendship, without conscience.

So the real question was never whether a person is allowed to love money. The real question is which chair money gets to sit in. Sitting where a tool belongs, it can do enormous good. The moment it climbs onto the throne, it starts running your life instead of the other way around.

⸻

## II. When "Work Hard" Quietly Becomes "Whatever Works"

My country went through an economic transformation few nations have matched in speed. The guiding instinct of that era was blunt and practical: it doesn't matter whether the cat is black or white, so long as it catches mice. Results talk. And there was real good in that — families who worked their way out of poverty, fathers who could finally feed their children well, young people who bought their first home with their own two hands. None of that deserves cynicism.

The trouble starts somewhere quieter: when "work hard and build a better life" slowly, almost without anyone noticing, curdles into "whatever makes money is fine." The two sentences look nearly identical on the page. Between them sits a canyon.

Once having money becomes the only ruler we measure success with, we start asking only how much, and stop asking how. The line keeps sliding. If cutting corners is profitable, someone cuts them. If a lie closes the deal, someone tells it. If the risk can be handed to somebody else while the reward stays home, somebody is usually willing.

Eventually you arrive at an absurdity that would be funny if it weren't so sad: the vegetable seller who won't eat his own vegetables, the butcher who won't touch his own meat, the food producer who won't let his own children eat what he makes. A society that reaches that point hasn't just lost some money. It has lost the one thing that actually holds people together — trust. And once trust is gone, everyone reaches for a little more, and somehow, everyone ends up more exhausted than before.

⸻

## III. Gaining the World, Losing the Self

Jesus once asked one of the sharpest questions ever put to a human being:

> For what shall it profit a man, if he shall gain the whole world, and lose his own soul? Or what shall a man give in exchange for his soul?
> — Mark 8:36, KJV

Two thousand years later, that question still won't let go. What, exactly, are you trading for what? We're practiced at tallying the number in the bank account. We rarely tally the other ledger. How much health did I spend to get here? How many of my child's small, unrepeatable years did the business swallow? How much distance crept into my marriage while I was building something else? Did my integrity get discounted along the way, a little at a time, without a single dramatic moment I could point to?

The worst outcome was never failing to make money. It's waking up one day to discover the money arrived, and you didn't — not really, not the version of you that mattered. Hence the joke with the bite in its tail: one of life's great tragedies is dying with money still in the bank.

We spend our youth trading health for money, then spend whatever's left of our age trying to trade the money back for health. Some things, it turns out, can be bought back. Others can't. Medicine, yes. Health itself, no. A bigger house, yes. The warmth that makes it a home, no. The best mattress money can find, yes. A night of actual rest, no. Insurance, yes. Tomorrow, no. Jesus never said money was worthless. He was warning us against something more specific: don't trade something priceless for something that merely has a price tag.

⸻

## IV. Wesley's Surprising Instruction: Go Make Money

At this point someone might conclude that money is simply too dangerous, and a person of faith should keep well clear of it. That's not where this goes. John Wesley preached a sermon that has outlived him by nearly three centuries, called simply *The Use of Money*. Its entire argument fits in three short lines:

**Gain all you can. Save all you can. Give all you can.**

I love how carefully balanced those three lines are. They don't romanticize poverty, and they don't worship wealth. Wesley opens, notably, with permission: gain all you can. A life of faith was never meant to be a life of idleness. Run the business well. Do the job with your whole heart. Keep sharpening the craft. Whatever ability you've been given, use it — don't let it rust from disuse.

But he draws the boundary immediately: not at the cost of your health, not at the cost of your soul, not at the cost of your neighbor. That single sentence changes the whole question. Not everything that can be earned should be. Not if it costs your health. Not if it only works through deception. Not if the profit is quietly built on someone else's loss. The believer's question was never just "does this pay?" It's "is this money earned rightly?"

⸻

## V. Learning to Make Money — and to Manage Wanting More

Wesley's second line is: save all you can. There's a trap built into this era of ours — income climbs, and somehow the feeling of having enough never arrives, because desire has a way of climbing right alongside it. The ordinary car that once felt like plenty starts to feel like a compromise. The house that once fit the family perfectly suddenly needs another wing.

Eventually you notice: what actually makes a person feel poor is rarely too little income. More often it's wanting too much. Saving isn't stinginess. It's a form of self-governance — I can spend, without being steered by the culture of spending. I can buy things, without needing the purchase to prove my worth. Real freedom was never having enough money to buy anything. It's knowing, clearly, that there are things you could easily afford and simply don't need.

⸻

## VI. The Last Line, and the Hardest One: Give All You Can

If Wesley had stopped at the first two lines — gain and save — the likely result is simply a wealthier person. Which is why the third line is the whole point of the sermon: give all you can.

Earning was never the finish line. Neither was saving. Money finds its real purpose when it becomes a blessing to somebody else — help for someone with nothing, support for work that matters, a hand extended to a family in trouble, an investment in the next generation, tuition paid, a gospel carried, a community served.

Something quietly remarkable happens at that point. The money stops being a number in an account and becomes a meal on someone's table, a book in a child's hands, a hospital bill covered, a missionary's plane ticket, the moment a family gets back on its feet. Money, on its own, has no pulse. But handled rightly, it becomes a channel life can travel through.

⸻

## VII. The Real Question Was Never "How Much Do You Have"

If I had to sum up everything I actually believe about money, I wouldn't say it doesn't matter — it plainly does; without it, ordinary life gets very hard, very fast. But I also wouldn't say it matters most, because the things that matter most in a life tend to be, precisely, the things money can't price.

The questions I'd rather sit with are quieter ones. Where does my money come from? Where does it go? What am I actually paying, in the parts of life that don't show up on a receipt, to get more of it? What have I done with what's been entrusted to me? And on the day I finally leave, will what's left behind be a number in an account, or people who were better off because I lived?

Scripture offers a view of wealth that cuts against almost everything the culture assumes: we were never owners. We were only ever stewards. The house, the savings, the career, the portfolio sitting in our hands today — every bit of it eventually leaves our hands. No one has ever carried a bank balance across that particular threshold. But we do get to decide, while it's still in our hands, what it's used for.

So — gain what you can, and don't sell your conscience to get it. Save what you can, and don't become a servant to what you've saved. And give, bravely, what you can — let the money pass through your hands long enough to become someone else's blessing on the way through.

Maybe real wealth was never about how much a person managed to hold onto over a lifetime. Maybe it's measured, instead, by how many people were better off because that person lived at all.

⸻

Money can buy almost anything. It was never meant to buy your soul. Earning is a skill. Managing is wisdom. Giving is a largeness of heart. And refusing to be owned by any of it — that is the only freedom worth having.
